Renting on Koh Samui as a newcomer: the complete relocation checklist

Samui Luxury Stays9 min read

Moving to Koh Samui is exciting, but the island is not a place where you can sort out a rental in an afternoon from your laptop. The people who settle in smoothly treat the move as a short project: decide what matters, see homes in person, read the lease carefully, and sort the practical details before they unpack. Here is a complete checklist, in the order it usually helps to tackle it.

1. Start earlier than you think

Good long-term rentals do not always sit on the market. Properties that suit families, remote workers and long-stay residents can be let quickly, and the best ones are often taken before they are widely advertised. If you can, begin your search two to three months before you plan to arrive, and keep your dates flexible by a few weeks.

2. Decide what matters most

Write down your non-negotiables before you look at a single photo. A short list saves weeks of wasted viewings:

  • Your monthly budget, including utilities, internet and pool or garden costs
  • The number of bedrooms, and whether you need a home office
  • Whether you need to be near an international school, the airport, a hospital or a gym
  • Beach access, or a quiet hillside with a view
  • Pets, parking and whether you will drive a car or a scooter

3. Choose your neighbourhood

Koh Samui is small, but its areas feel very different. Bophut and Fisherman’s Village are lively and convenient for the airport. Choeng Mon, Plai Laem and Bang Rak suit people who want a calm base with good services nearby. Chaweng and Lamai are the busiest, with the most shops and nightlife. Maenam and Ban Tai are quieter and relaxed, while Lipa Noi, Taling Ngam and the south and west coasts offer peace and sunsets, with more driving. Nathon is the island’s main town rather than a beach resort. Our area guide sets out each neighbourhood in more detail, and many areas are not walkable, so think about how you will get around.

4. See homes in person, or have someone do it for you

Photos flatter. What listings rarely show is noise from a road or a neighbour, how the home copes after heavy rain, the real internet speed, the state of the air-conditioning, and how far it really is from the things you need. If you cannot be on the island, a trusted person on the ground who inspects each property and sends you honest photos and video is worth far more than another week of browsing listings.

5. What to check at every viewing

  • Air-conditioning, plumbing, hot water and electrics all working properly
  • Signs of damp, mould or roof leaks, especially after the rainy season
  • Internet speed and mobile signal
  • Pool and garden condition, and who looks after them
  • Noise, neighbours, road access and safe parking
  • Drainage and flood risk, if the home sits low
  • That the furniture and appliances match what is listed

6. Read the lease and the deposit terms

Long-term leases on the island commonly run for six to twelve months, and shorter rentals usually cost more per month. A refundable security deposit plus the first month in advance is a common arrangement, but terms vary, so always ask. Before you sign, check who pays for repairs, pool and garden care, and utilities, how much notice either side must give, what happens if you leave early, and how and when the rent can be increased. Get everything in writing, and keep copies.

7. Understand utilities and running costs

Ask whether electricity and water are billed at the standard utility rate or marked up by the landlord. Air-conditioning is usually the biggest electricity cost, so ask for recent bills. Confirm that fibre internet is available at the address, not just in the area, particularly if you work remotely.

8. Sort the paperwork

Foreign tenants are normally reported to Thai immigration by the landlord or host through the TM30 notification, so ask your landlord about it. Keep copies of your passport, visa and lease together. Visas and work permits are a separate subject and the rules change, so take advice from a licensed immigration adviser rather than relying on forums.

9. Plan how you will get around

Most of the island is not walkable. Many residents use a car or a scooter. If you plan to drive, make sure you hold the right licence, since an International Driving Permit is generally needed to drive legally. Taxis and ride-hailing exist but can be costly for daily use.

10. Think about the seasons

The island is generally hot and dry from roughly February to August, with its wetter months typically running from about October to December. If you arrive in the rainy season, check the roof, the drainage and the access road, and look closely for damp.

11. Arrange healthcare and insurance

Private hospitals on the island offer good, English-speaking care. Make sure your health insurance covers treatment in Thailand before you need it, rather than after.

12. Protect yourself from rental scams

Fake owners and fake agents are a genuine and common problem on Koh Samui, and people searching from overseas are the easiest targets. Never pay a deposit for a place you have not seen, and only pay against a signed lease and a written receipt. We cover this in detail in our guide to rental scams on Koh Samui.

The smoothest moves are not the luckiest ones. They are the ones where someone checked the details before any money changed hands.

In short

A good relocation comes down to a clear brief, honest viewings, a carefully read lease, and a few practical details handled early. Take it step by step, and the island will reward you. For the wider picture on visas, costs and everyday life, see our first-timer’s guide to island life.

This article is general information, not legal, tax or immigration advice. Rules and costs can change, so please confirm current requirements before you travel.
